The Eerie Genesis and Lifespan of a Book

Cyropaedia was considered, at one point, essential reading for statesmen like Thomas Jefferson, and is part of a strange and scattershot coterie of books that have had a mysterious lifespan; at least more mysterious than the average collection of bound tree-debris the fondest reader calls her library. These are books that might have done poorly upon publication but are now considered classics, a part of a national syllabus even. They can be books that didn't get published until the author's death. That were censored, or suppressed, or "unpublishable". And then there are those books that shot straight into an unsuspecting sky and exploded like a bright band of fireworks. And then just as quickly disappeared, and everyone scratches their heads with vague memories of a title and bare outlines of a plot (or scandal - if current publishing models persist). The latter scenario is a more extreme version of the very fate of Cyropaedia. Not even the avid reader today is as familiar with Cyrus the way she may be with Machiavelli.

There's arguably one thing that Norman Mailer was correct about during his life and that is that writing is "the spooky art". With or without inborn talent, a writer with a strong work ethic can enter a headspace unchartered by anyone else. Fiction, in particular, bears the hallmarks of this spookiness. The best analogy I've drawn up is: imagine you are driving a car, and are suddenly "struck", as if with lightning or an oncoming car, with some sensation/idea/ person/phrase/ sentence/a fully-fledged story in some cases, you cannot help but sit frozen still, you keep the door ajar just to see what happens, and pretty soon, characters and accents and events and details come flooding into this vehicle and you cannot and dare not make them leave. They will stay with you, for as long as it takes. Sometimes years. And you will oblige them with your full attention.

No system including, and perhaps especially, the traditional book publishing model and, still yet, the digital book one, has ever managed to capture the queer elusiveness of the very product it is peddling. No system has ever even tried to keep time with its complex-rhythms, its inherent and unique potential of enabling the drawing up of often-outlandish worlds with precision, or simply relaying complex research and ideas, as communication between what are often vastly diverse consciousnesses. This potential however, and for whatever equally mysterious reason, usually remains just that: potential. One that is rarely if ever actuated or even explored by an era's actively publishing authors. Even by its most talented.

The nature of a book, with its often distinct anti-utilitarian-ness, and the nature of the novel in particular, hence "a novel idea", has the capacity to bear disregard and disenchantment with convention, for what has come to pass before it, for the following of fads or unredeemed cliché. The best of them actively rebel. The only true success in this, if we can call it the "logic of a book" or the "time signature of a book", is staying true and alive to itself, and its many composite elements. To what is being born through the midwife of its author. But that truth, it seems, is antagonistic to the old and current systems that encapsulate this actually rather fledgling technology we call "a book".

Whether from a millennia ago or a month ago, compelled by the ineffable, the book-creator anticipates, though is unruffled and almost always oblivious (not enough mental space to go around, I suppose) to what may come after her creation.

~HS
