“Geographic Energy Alignment”


“Emotional Geography” is a new topic in the study of human geography, and its newness is reflected in this skeletal wiki: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_geography

It seems self-evident that people carry different emotional reactions, often dramatic ones, when exposed to different locations (cities, towns, venues, countries); becoming slightly different people in different places. Or maybe using the “energy” of a place — as an activator (or excuse) — to be in or out of alignment with the best/worst version of ourselves. I’ve always been curious, though, as to why there is so little literature on the subject, let alone corresponding and adequate terms? Why do we feel attracted to certain places more than others? Why do I feel a strong pull to visit Mongolia or Ireland or Ethiopia and others do not? Why do some of us still feel the “energetic residue” of early twentieth-century artists and writers in the streets of Paris? Feel hauntingly bad juju in New England? And others do not?

Can people experience heightened states of love, success, depression, creativity — scattered or concentrated in different places in the world? Is the likelihood of finding others of like mind increased when you have – or increasingly notice – these shifts?  What is it called when you like who you become, or what it brings out of you, in such places? Just “love”? What is it called when you don’t? Just “hate”? Seems a little facile for a species that’s been traveling and recording its travels for some time.

Why do we not pay more attention to it when we experience it? Even most contemporary travel writing is devoid of any mention. If someone expresses curiosity or affection for a particular location on Earth (or off Earth), we reflexively scoff. We call it “fantasy” as if it by calling it such, we can automatically erode its legitimacy or significance as a part of a human experience, or even human evolution.

The amount of mockery around this subject is surprising, which is partly why I think it’s so interesting to explore. The most common retort I hear is “the sky is the same color everywhere”, presumably an attempt at sounding wise. In certain places, like smog-blanketed Beijing, for instance, such a response happens to be literally untrue. Perhaps it is more stoic to ignore subtle and sometimes-not-so-subtle shifts in how location (which includes culture, history, etc.) can affect emotion and vice versa — but it doesn’t seem to be very fruitful.

Maybe a more evolved future classroom would encourage people to explore their “geographic pulse points” as one of the requisites of becoming a self-aware adult. And maybe the most productive areas of study (and creativity) in the coming years will be the ones that are cross-culturally mocked, seen as foolish, or, simply – the ones with one-liner wikis.
